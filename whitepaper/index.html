Whitepaper

Rawl

Rawl shows notes as colored bars. Position shows time and pitch. Color shows the relationship to a chosen tonic. Shape helps you follow a part.

01

Reading notes

Time

Read from left to right. A bar starts when the note starts and ends when it ends. A longer bar lasts longer. A gap means silence in that part. Notes above one another happen together.

Example: a one-second note is twice as wide as a half-second note at the same zoom.

Pitch

Higher pitches appear higher. Equal pitch distances take equal vertical space. C to C♯ is one semitone. C to D is two. C to E is four. C to G is seven. C to the next C is twelve: an octave.

An interval is the distance between pitches. Note height changes the vertical zoom without changing the sound. A bar can grow taller during playback or hover. That is a highlight, not a louder note.

Shapes

Different corner shapes distinguish parts. Match them to the voice controls. A melody and bass can share a color while keeping different shapes. A shape has no fixed instrument meaning across pieces. Grouped parts with the same instrument name can share one shape and control.

02

Colors

Tonic

The tonic is the pitch used as home. It is white in every octave. Other colors show distances from it. With C as tonic, C is white. With D as tonic, D is white.

The numbers name positions above the tonic. A flat lowers a numbered position by one semitone. A sharp raises it by one semitone.

Palette

With C as tonic:

  • 1 · C · white
  • ♭2 · D♭ · dark red · white inside dot
  • 2 · D · red
  • ♭3 · E♭ · dark green
  • 3 · E · bright green
  • 4 · F · purple
  • ♯4 · F♯ · light purple · black inside dot
  • 5 · G · gray
  • ♭6 · A♭ · blue
  • 6 · A · cyan
  • ♭7 · B♭ · orange
  • 7 · B · yellow

These colors repeat in every octave. C–E–G in C and D–F♯–A in D share the same white–green–gray pattern.

Color pairs

Neighboring alternatives have related colors: ♭2–2, ♭3–3, 4–♯4, ♭6–6, and ♭7–7. Orange and yellow form the last pair.

Example: C minor has E♭, a dark green third. C major has E, a bright green third. The tonic stays white.

Chord flags

A chord flag is the group of colors made by a chord. Its notes can sound together, arrive one after another, or spread across octaves.

The palette follows thirds: ♭2, 2, 4, ♯4, ♭6, 6, 1, ♭3, 3, 5, ♭7, 7. Many common chords stack thirds, so their colors form smooth, related flags.

Example: C–E–G is white–green–gray in C. G–B–D is gray–yellow–red. Moving a note by an octave keeps its color. An inversion changes the vertical order but keeps the same set of colors.

Green and red

Green helps identify tonic triads. Their third is bright green in major and dark green in minor. Both also contain white and gray.

Red appears in the usual dominant chord. In C, G–B–D contains gray, yellow, and red. Adding F gives the dominant seventh chord.

When G7 leads to C, B can rise to C and D can fall to C. Yellow and red arrive at white. These are useful clues. One green or red note alone does not identify a chord’s function.

Inside dots

♭2 has a white dot inside its bar. ♯4 has a black dot. They help distinguish neighboring colors in small notes. The dots follow the colored position when the tonic changes.

Example: D♭ has the white inside dot in C. With D♭ as tonic, it becomes white and loses the dot. Small tuning offsets near these positions keep the inside dot. It is not an accent or a rhythm mark.

Pitch bends

A pitch between piano keys appears between the usual semitone positions. Its color blends the neighboring colors. A fixed bend gives a bar at its sounding height. A changing bend gives a ribbon that moves vertically over time.

Example: a note rising from D toward E♭ rises on the page and blends red toward dark green when C is tonic. Rawl shows pitch movement stored in the source.

Arabic quartertones

A quartertone lies halfway between two semitone positions. Arabic pitches also vary with the maqam and performance; they need not sit exactly halfway.

Rawl uses the stored sounding height and a blended color. A small white dot outside the note marks an intermediate position. It goes below for ♭2–2, ♭3–3, 4–♯4, ♭6–6, and ♭7–7. It goes above for the other gaps.

Example: halfway between E♭ and E is blended green with a dot below. Halfway between D and E♭ has a red-and-green blend with a dot above. Both examples use C as tonic.

Inside dots distinguish ♭2 and ♯4. Outside dots mark intermediate pitches. Near ♭2 or ♯4, the inside dot takes priority.

03

Changing tonic

Recoloring

Recoloring chooses a new tonic for the same notes. Pitch and timing stay the same. Colors change because the reference changes.

Example: G–B–D is gray–yellow–red in C and white–green–gray in G. Transposing moves sounding pitches. Recoloring changes how you read them.

Modulation

A modulation establishes a new tonal home. An annotated tonic change recolors the passage and changes its tonal grid.

Example: a piece begins in C and settles in G. G changes from gray to white. B changes from yellow to bright green. The annotation records a reading of the music.

Tonicization

Tonicization gives a chord a brief local role as home. The music can then return to the main tonic.

Example: in C, D7 leads to G before the music returns to C. G briefly acts as a local tonic. The boundary between tonicization and modulation depends on context. Rawl can show either with a local tonic change.

Applied chords

An applied chord points toward a local target. D7 contains D–F♯–A–C and often leads to G. Relative to G, it is an ordinary dominant seventh chord. In C, we call it V7/V: the dominant seventh of the dominant.

E7 leading to A minor in C is V7/vi. The slash names the target. Uppercase numerals name major chords; lowercase numerals name minor chords. The chord symbol 7 adds a seventh above its root.

Two readings

Keep C as tonic for D7–G and learn the applied chord’s flag. You see its place in the larger key. F♯ has the light purple inside dot.

Or recolor the passage in G. You see familiar dominant-to-tonic flags. F♯ becomes yellow and G becomes white.

Keeping one tonic helps you follow the larger route. Recoloring helps you recognize a local pattern. A short applied chord may be easier to learn as a flag. A longer passage may be easier to read after recoloring. Both readings have the same sound.

04

Tonal grid

The tonal grid gives pitch landmarks behind the notes. Shaded bands mark tonic octaves. Dashed lines mark fifths between them.

Example: in C, the grid repeats around C and marks G. In G, it repeats around G and marks D. Changing the tonic changes the grid and colors. The sounding pitches keep their heights.

05

Rhythm and form

Measures

Beats are pulse positions used to count time. Measures, or bars, group beats. Count a four-beat bar as 1 2 3 4. The next bar begins a new 1. Numbered vertical lines mark measures. Lighter internal lines can mark beats. Bar width shows duration, so bars need not be equally wide.

Beat grouping

Beat grouping shows smaller groups inside a measure. Five units can be 3+2: 1 2 3 | 1 2. Ten units can be 3+2+2+3. The internal grid marks group boundaries. Grouping changes the guide lines, not playback. It continues until changed or reset.

Phrases

A phrase is a musical span that can feel like one thought. Four measures are a useful starting point. Rawl begins with phrase guides at measures 1, 5, 9, and 13. These guides are editable.

Music can diverge from this pattern. A four-bar phrase can gain an extra bar. The next phrase then begins at 6 instead of 5. Later guides can shift with it. Phrase lengths should follow the music.

Sections

A section groups a larger passage, such as a verse or chorus. It can contain several phrases. Rawl can separate sections into blocks for comparison. Each can have a name, tonic changes, and different phrase lengths.

Example: compare a verse and chorus to find a repeated melody or changed ending.

Anchoring

Anchoring shifts a section sideways to align its start or end with a phrase in another section.

Example: one section omits the first four bars of another. Align its start with the longer section’s second phrase. Their shared material lines up. End alignment helps compare endings of different lengths.

Anchoring changes layout. It does not add silence or change playback order.

Real tempo

Equal seconds take equal horizontal space at the same zoom. Acceleration brings successive beats closer together. Slowing down spreads them apart. A held ending can make a bar much wider.

Example: four quarter-note beats at 120 beats per minute last two seconds. At 60, they last four seconds and take twice the width. Expressive timing is visible when the source includes it.

Time spacing changes horizontal zoom. Playback speed changes listening speed. Neither rewrites the timing drawn in the score.

06

Omitted marks

Enharmonic spelling

C♯ and D♭ can name the same pitch on an equal-tempered piano. Rawl gives that pitch one height and one color for the chosen tonic. F♯ and G♭ likewise share a position. In C, the legend calls it ♯4.

Legend names label pitch positions. They do not preserve every spelling in a source score. Actual differences in sounding pitch remain visible when the source stores them.

Volume and expression

Colored bars do not carry ordinary staff marks such as p, f, crescendos, or expressive directions. Their thickness does not show loudness.

Example: a soft C and a loud C can look the same. Playback can still differ if the source stores different velocities or volume controls. Pitch height and bend ribbons show stored pitch, without separate instructions to play sharper, flatter, louder, or with a particular expression.
